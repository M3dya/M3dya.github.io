Case Study · Self-Initiated

One split role model, eight points of silent failure

Autodesk customers couldn’t predict what they’d be charged or fix a mistake before money moved. I traced the failure to its root — a split account-role model — and redesigned the renewal journey around it.

Note on this project

This is a self-initiated case study built from public complaint patterns and Autodesk’s own account documentation — not internal access or an Autodesk affiliation. Treat it as a demonstration of process, not a claim of shipped impact.

Why This Happened

Three different people, one silent charge

Autodesk accounts routinely split across three roles: the person who buys the seats at checkout, the admin who manages the account day to day, and whoever’s card actually gets charged at renewal. Nothing in the product keeps those three roles looking at the same information at the same time.

That gap doesn’t show up as one dramatic failure — it shows up as eight small, compounding ones, each quiet enough to miss until the money has already moved.

Challenges

Eight stages, eight silent failures

Mapping the current-state journey end to end surfaced a friction point at every single stage — not one root failure, but a pattern repeating in a new form each time:

StageCurrent-state friction
01 CheckoutSeats & term look identical
02 OnboardingAdmin has no billing view
03 Renewal WindowNotice goes to one inbox
04 Auto-ChargeSilence read as consent
05 DiscoveryWrong role finds the charge
06 Support ContactPolicy applied, no exception
07 CancellationChat “done” isn’t done
08 OutcomeTrust lost, repeat contact
A renewal notice goes to one inbox, silence at that inbox gets read as consent, and the charge lands on a card whoever’s watching it didn’t expect — discovered by whichever role happens to check first, weeks later.
Behaviour Understanding

The account has three users. The product assumes one.

Every friction point above traces back to the same behavioural mismatch: the buyer, the admin, and the billing contact each interact with the account at a different moment, and none of them see what the others saw. A design that treats “the customer” as a single person will always leave two of the three roles out of the loop.

Design implication

The fix isn’t better copy on the renewal email — it’s making status visible to every role that touches the account, not just whichever one happened to be cc’d.

Design Process

Redesigning around visibility, not notifications

Rather than patch each stage individually, I applied one principle consistently across all eight: every role should be able to see the same status, at the same time, without needing to be the one who got the email.

StageRedesigned resolution
01 CheckoutPlain-language confirmation
02 OnboardingSecondary contact added
03 Renewal WindowStatus visible to all roles
04 Auto-ChargeChanges flagged pre-charge
05 DiscoveryHealth shown to every role
06 Support ContactAgents empowered to fix
07 CancellationOne action, visible progress
08 OutcomeResolved on first contact

Visibility over notification

A status any role can check beats a notice only one role receives.

Flag before charging

Changes surface pre-charge, not as a surprise after the card is billed.

Resolve on first contact

Support agents get the authority to fix, not just apply policy.

Outcome

Same eight stages, redesigned system

The redesign doesn’t add new stages — it resolves the same eight that already existed, replacing a friction point with a visible resolution at each one.
